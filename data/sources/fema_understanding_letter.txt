Understanding Your FEMA Letter
Source: FEMA.gov, https://www.fema.gov/assistance/individual/after-applying/understanding-letter (last updated June 26, 2026). U.S. government work.

After you apply for help from FEMA, you will get an introduction letter that explains the types of help FEMA provides. FEMA will also send other letters that explain what money you are receiving and what documents you need to send if FEMA is missing information needed to move your application forward.

What to Look For

Read the whole letter carefully. The top things to look for in your FEMA letter are:
- Your Disaster Number and Application Number will be at the top of your letter. Save this information as you will need it later.
- Pay attention to bolded words for key information.
- Look for any dates or timeframes.
- Pay attention to where it says "OR" in required documents, as only one document from a long list may be needed.
- Check for additional pages or forms, since letters often come with additional forms that may need to be completed.

Note: You still may be eligible for FEMA assistance even if your insurance doesn't cover all of your losses or expenses. FEMA cannot give you money for what your insurance already paid for.

If FEMA Needs More Information from You

Sometimes FEMA needs additional information like an insurance settlement, a copy of a receipt for disaster costs, or documents that confirm your identity and where you live. Your FEMA letter will explain exactly which documents you need to send and how to submit them. For example, you may need to send documents that show:
- Insurance settlement or denial: what your insurance does or doesn't cover.
- Social Security Number (SSN): to confirm your identity. You may send documents that show your full SSN or documents from other federal agencies that show the last four digits of your SSN.
- Occupancy: that you live in your home most of the year.
- Ownership: that you own or are responsible for your home most of the year.
- Estimates or receipts: your costs from the disaster.

FEMA's Decisions

Approved: your letter will explain how much money you will get and what the money can be used for. Read your entire letter, as it explains how to ask for more help if you need it.

Not approved: your letter will explain what to do if you disagree with FEMA's decision. You can send more information to show why you need help, like repair estimates or an insurance settlement, and FEMA will take another look at your case. Look for the section labeled "Your Next Steps" to find out which documents FEMA needs from you.

Appeal Form

At the end of your letter, look for an appeal form that can be used to send additional information about your situation. You may also download the appeal form. If it's easier to just write a letter, that's fine too. Sending an appeal form or letter with your additional information is optional.

Additional Help

If you need more help understanding your FEMA letter, you can:
- Look for information and status updates about your case at DisasterAssistance.gov.
- Visit a Disaster Recovery Center (DRC) for in-person help. Find the nearest DRC at fema.gov/drc.
- Call FEMA's Helpline at 1-800-621-3362.
